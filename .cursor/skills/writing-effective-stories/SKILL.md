# Writing Effective Stories

Use stories to give an audience a concrete reason to care, not to decorate an argument that lacks one. Base every story on supplied or verifiable experience. When the needed facts are missing, ask for them or use clearly marked placeholders—never manufacture a person, event, quotation, statistic, or outcome.

## Choose the story's job

Use the smallest story that serves the communication goal. The following six story jobs are useful starting points:

| Goal | Story job | What it establishes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Earn trust or make the speaker relatable | **Who I am** | Relevant lived experience, perspective, or credibility |
| Explain commitment or motive | **Why I'm here** | Why this work, decision, or audience matters now |
| Help people imagine a change | **Vision** | A tangible, believable future after the change |
| Make a principle usable | **Teaching** | A situation, decision, and lesson the audience can apply |
| Demonstrate a standard | **Values in action** | What the value required when it was costly or unclear |
| Address resistance without becoming defensive | **I know what you're thinking** | Acknowledgment of a reasonable concern and evidence that answers it |

If more than one job applies, lead with the one that removes the audience's main barrier. Do not force all six into one piece.

## Build a story with stakes

Prefer a specific moment over a summary of achievements. Give the reader enough of the following to follow an unfolding change:

1. **Situation:** who was there, where they were, and what was at stake.
2. **Tension:** the obstacle, uncertainty, tradeoff, or unmet need.
3. **Choice:** what someone did—or chose not to do—and why.
4. **Result:** what changed, including an honest limit or unresolved consequence when relevant.
5. **Meaning:** one sentence that connects the event to the audience's decision, value, or next step.

Open near the moment of tension. Use concrete, source-supported details that let the audience infer the point. Name the lesson after the event has earned it; do not begin with a moral and then retrofit an anecdote.

## Match form to the material

- A short speech, email, or presentation usually needs one scene and one takeaway.
- A case study can use several scenes, but should still center the people and decisions rather than a feature list.
- A vision story should make the future observable: what a person can do, avoid, notice, or feel differently.
- A skeptical audience needs its concern stated fairly before the response. Do not create a straw objection.
- If the source material is thin, write a concise factual example instead of pretending it is a dramatic story.

## Drafting and revision rules

- Keep the narrator and point of view appropriate to the source. Do not put first-person experience in a speaker's mouth unless it is documented.
- Preserve confidentiality, consent, and dignity. Generalize or anonymize identifying details when needed without implying false precision.
- Let actions, dialogue, and decisions carry the value claim. Remove adjectives that merely announce that something was inspiring, transformative, unique, or impactful.
- Retain uncertainty and imperfect outcomes when they are material; credibility is more persuasive than a neat ending.
- Connect the final insight to a clear audience implication: understand, decide, try, support, or change something.

Before delivering, verify that the story has a clear job, a real source, a visible turning point, and a takeaway proportionate to the evidence. If revising existing prose, preserve the author's facts and voice unless the user requests a substantive change.
